# Short drama

Start with intended audience and tone, the thematic core, what characters want, what obstructs them, and the choices that create consequences. Establish the series/episode scope and what has actually been produced.

For an outline, map desire → obstacle → choice → consequence → changed situation. Identify reversals without groundwork, actions contradicted by established motivation, information revealed too late for comprehension, and repeated conflict without progression. Do not replace every story with a high-arousal twist formula.

For scenes, point to dialogue, action, or scene IDs. For footage, record actual inspected timestamps before discussing performance, continuity, or sound. A script can have plausible character causality while the performance remains untested.

Each episode should offer a meaningful local payoff and a justified reason to continue, according to the chosen form. Cliffhangers are not a substitute for satisfying development. A deliberately quiet ending can fit an expression goal.

Offer alternatives that protect the theme: reduce a world mechanic, reorder revelation, strengthen a motive, or boldly restructure parallel character lines. Explain downstream consequences and production cost; do not assume smaller is always better.

Example experiment: use a reading or rough scene to check whether intended viewers can explain a character's desire, choice, and consequence. Ask why they would or would not continue. Stated intent is not actual continuation. If no audience exists, perform a motive/causality pass and label audience comprehension unknown.

AI tradeoffs include character/voice consistency, continuity across shots and episodes, difficult revisions to established material, emotional credibility, and maintaining creative authorship. Numerous generated scenes may add workload without advancing relationships.

Feedback: distinguish “I did not understand the choice” from “I dislike this genre” and “the performance did not convey the choice.” Each calls for a different change.
