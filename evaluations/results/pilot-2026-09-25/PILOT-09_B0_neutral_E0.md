The remark is the main question to resolve. Cruelty can reveal anxiety without a breakdown: a person under pressure may lash out to regain control or deflect attention. But if the scene hasn’t prepared that reaction, readers may experience it as an unexplained change in character. You don’t necessarily need to soften the line; you need to find out whether its emotional cause is legible.

Give the current scene to three readers without explaining the intended emotion. Ask them independently:

- What is the protagonist trying to prevent or hide?
- What do you think prompts the cruel remark?
- Where, if anywhere, did their behavior stop making sense?

Ask each reader to point to a line or action supporting their answer. This will be more useful than asking whether the anxiety was clear, which supplies the interpretation you’re trying to test.

If readers understand the fear but lose the connection at the remark, revise only that beat: add or sharpen a small trigger before it and a restrained consequence afterward. For example, a failed attempt to sound casual could precede the attack; an aborted apology or a return to rehearsing could follow it. Choose behavior that fits this protagonist rather than adding an explanatory speech.

Keep the understated approach. First test whether the missing piece is motivation, then spend revision effort there.
