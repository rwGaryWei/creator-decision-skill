The strongest idea is the overlap between categories: deciding where a book belongs could make sorting feel like interpretation rather than clerical work. The main design risk is pairing that ambiguity with a single correct-answer check. Players may make a reasonable choice and be told they are wrong without learning anything useful.

With design notes alone, the concept is promising, but its playfulness is unproven. Your next investment should be a small paper prototype that tests the sorting rule and feedback before you build more content or presentation.

Make one puzzle with eight book cards and three labeled shelves. Include two books that plausibly belong on more than one shelf. Choose the rule explicitly:

- If the pleasure is finding a unique solution, give players visible constraints that explain why only one arrangement works—for example, shelf capacity and a reader’s request.
- If the pleasure is interpreting categories, accept multiple defensible placements and let choices have understandable consequences.

I would test the second approach first because it uses your overlapping categories directly. Give each book visible attributes, allow placement wherever the stated shelf criteria fit, and add a reader request that makes some valid arrangements more useful than others. This gives players a reason to reconsider their sorting without declaring their interpretation invalid.

Try it with three people who have not seen the notes. Ask them to explain one ambiguous placement, then watch what happens when they receive feedback. Do they understand their options and want to try another arrangement, or do they hunt for your preferred answer?

Invest in a playable digital version once this small puzzle produces understandable choices and voluntary experimentation. If it does not, revise the category rules and feedback first; additional books will only multiply the uncertainty.
