Your next investment should be a small, playable sorting exercise that tests how the game treats ambiguous books. The concept has a useful source of play—overlapping categories—but a single correct-answer check could undermine it if players make reasonable placements that the game rejects.

Based on the brief, the only evidence is the design notes: categories overlap, validation accepts one answer, and there is no playable build. That does not establish that the puzzle is confusing or enjoyable. It identifies the first design risk to test.

A library is a promising setting because players can bring their own associations to titles, subjects, and genres. For an audience that enjoys interpretation and organizing, discovering multiple sensible relationships could be the pleasure. An audience seeking deduction may instead want precise rules and one provable solution. Those are audience hypotheses; choosing which experience matters most will help you decide what “correct” means.

Keep categorization central, and compare two approaches:

- **Explicit library rules:** Retain one solution, but give players enough information to deduce it. For example, each librarian could prioritize a different property. This offers clear puzzle progression, although it risks turning expressive sorting into rule compliance.
- **Several valid arrangements:** Accept placements that satisfy stated category conditions, including overlaps. This supports experimentation, but needs feedback that explains relationships and constraints so sorting still feels purposeful.

I would test the second approach first because it uses the overlap already present in your concept. The first remains a useful comparison, particularly if you want compact deduction puzzles.

Make one paper or clickable puzzle with about 10 books, three shelves, and a few intentionally ambiguous items. Give each book short, readable metadata so players reason from available information rather than guessing what a title means. Prepare two validation versions using the same content: your single-answer version and a version accepting several coherent arrangements. Paper cards and a human facilitator are sufficient; polished art and a full game system can wait.

Ask five prospective players to try both versions, alternating which comes first. Record their initial placements and explanations before revealing feedback. Watch for defensible answers being rejected, requests for hidden rules, and whether feedback leads to another interesting attempt. Afterwards, ask which version they would voluntarily play another round of and why. This is a small qualitative test, not proof of audience demand.

The prototype is feasible at this scale. The harder production work would come later: maintaining consistent categories, explaining edge cases, and making new puzzles meaningfully different. If you use AI, use it to draft candidate books and category combinations, then check every item manually. Generation cost is only part of the expense; editing, ambiguity review, and testing may dominate. Avoid a live AI judge for this first test: variable rulings would make it harder to tell whether the underlying puzzle works.

Invest in more content only after players can understand the feedback and find the sorting itself worth repeating. If reasonable answers repeatedly fail the check, revise validation. If sorting is clear but dull, add a competing constraint—such as limited shelf space—and test again before expanding the library.
