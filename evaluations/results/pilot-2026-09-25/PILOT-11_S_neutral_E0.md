**Recommendation: invest your next small work session in a paper-playable sorting puzzle and its feedback rules.** Defer building more puzzles until you can show how overlapping categories produce an interesting decision and how the game explains its verdict.

**Preserve:** Making categorization playful. Overlap could be the source of that play: one book can belong in several places, so players must interpret clues, balance constraints, or discover a librarian’s peculiar organizing logic.

**Why:** Your brief reports overlapping categories and a single correct-answer check. That combination offers a promising tension, but also the main risk: a reasonable placement might be rejected simply because it differs from the designer’s answer. This is a design inference, not an observed playtest failure. A single solution can work well if the rules make it understandable and discoverable.

The decisive unknown is **what makes one placement better than another**. Is the player solving explicit constraints, discovering a hidden organizing rule, or inventing a defensible classification? Choose that before investing in implementation.

There are three viable directions:

- **A constrained arrangement:** Keep overlapping categories, but add an understandable constraint that resolves them—for example, each book needs a home and each shelf has limited space. This supports a single solution if you construct it carefully. The cost is puzzle-authoring and checking effort; too many constraints could crowd out the playful categorization.
- **Several valid arrangements:** Accept any sorting that satisfies stated rules. This preserves interpretive freedom and invites rearrangement, but needs a way to evaluate validity beyond matching one stored answer.
- **Discover the librarian’s rule:** Keep one intended answer and make learning an eccentric taxonomy the puzzle. Each failed attempt must reveal useful information. This can add personality and surprise, but weak clues would turn discovery into guessing.

My default would be to start with **a constrained arrangement** because it can test your current single-answer approach with little additional scope. Multiple valid arrangements remain a meaningful alternative if expressive interpretation matters more to you than deduction.

**Next experiment:** Make one version, “Paper puzzle 1,” with six book cards, three shelves, a short visible rule sheet, and at least two books that plausibly fit multiple shelves. Use whatever book descriptions you already have; no polished assets are needed. Cap the first pass at about two hours as a proposed effort limit.

Before showing it to anyone, list the plausible arrangements and check whether your rules actually distinguish the intended solution. Design feedback for one understandable wrong attempt: it should identify a violated rule or supply a clue that supports a different next move.

If a willing player is available, let them sort without coaching. Observe their first ambiguous choice, their explanation, what they learn from feedback, and whether they choose to attempt another arrangement. Ask what made the sorting enjoyable or frustrating. One player can expose a problem; they cannot establish broad appeal. If no player is available, do the same checks yourself and leave enjoyment and comprehension by others unverified.
