Your next investment should be a small, observed playtest of the exploration loop. The synthetic notes give you a reason to investigate backtracking, but they do not establish that real players find it frustrating. The blanket criticism of AI writing is also not evidence about this game's dialogue quality.

The goal—reward exploration without exhaustive dialogue—is coherent. A mailbox adventure could make discovery feel personal through letters, objects, locations, and visible changes, with optional text adding depth. That is a promising design direction, not a demonstrated strength of the current build: I have not seen the game or observed anyone playing it.

For players who enjoy discovery but prefer little reading, the key needs are knowing enough to choose a destination, finding something worth the trip, and recognizing what changed. Backtracking can support those needs if a familiar mailbox acquires a new meaning or reward. It can undermine them if players repeatedly check unchanged locations because they cannot tell where progress is possible. The notes do not tell us which is happening.

I would compare three modest options before expanding the game:

- **Make revisits legible:** use a visible mailbox state, short clue, or world change to indicate new content. This preserves exploration but risks making discovery too explicit.
- **Make revisits worthwhile:** add a meaningful change or discovery along an existing return route. This can enrich exploration, but increases content and consistency work.
- **Reduce empty travel:** add a shortcut after the first visit. This lowers repetition with relatively little new content, though it will not fix unclear objectives.

Choose among them based on observed behavior. Avoid compensating for unclear navigation with more mandatory dialogue; that would work against your goal.

For the next experiment, use a short existing section with a few mailboxes, one necessary revisit, and at least one optional discovery. Recruit roughly five people who enjoy exploration games and ask them to play without coaching. Record where they go, which unchanged locations they recheck, when they hesitate, whether they find the optional reward, and whether they can explain their next move. Afterwards, ask which trips felt rewarding and which felt unnecessary. This is a small qualitative test, not a representative estimate of your audience.

Then change only the most plausible cause and try the section with a few new players. If people lose track of the destination, test a concise cue. If they know where to go but dislike the journey, test a shortcut. If they arrive easily but feel unrewarded, improve the discovery itself. Look for fewer purposeless checks while preserving voluntary exploration and comprehension without required extra reading.

If you use AI for writing, drafting more letters may be cheap, but selecting, editing, checking clues, maintaining character consistency, and implementing each letter still cost time. Generating variants now could multiply that burden without addressing the underlying problem. Use a few deliberately edited pieces for the test and track total production time, including review. Revisit writing quality through specific player reactions to clarity, voice, and emotional payoff—not through a general verdict on AI.

I would defer additional locations and a broad dialogue rewrite until this test identifies whether the limiting factor is direction, travel, or reward.
