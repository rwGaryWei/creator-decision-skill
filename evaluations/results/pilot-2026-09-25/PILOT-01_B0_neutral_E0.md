The idea has a clear purpose: make shift exchanges easier while keeping the organizer responsible for approval. It is worth a small prototype, but there is not yet enough evidence to justify building a full scheduling tool. With no roster connection or recruited volunteers, the main uncertainty is whether a structured request can reduce email coordination without creating confusion about who is actually assigned.

Your next investment should be one complete, organizer-reviewed swap request—not a marketplace for finding replacements. For the first prototype, assume the requester already has a proposed replacement. That keeps the two-day scope manageable, though it leaves replacement discovery untested.

Build this narrow flow:

1. A volunteer enters their shift, proposed replacement, and a short note.
2. The replacement explicitly accepts the proposal.
3. The organizer reviews the details and approves or declines it.
4. Both volunteers see the decision. Until approval, the original assignment remains in effect.

Because there is no roster API, show shift details as supplied by the requester and require the organizer to verify them against the existing roster. Include a visible step for recording an approved change in that roster; approval alone must not imply that the schedule has been updated.

Spend the first half-day mapping this flow and drafting the screens, then the remaining build time making the end-to-end interaction demonstrable with fictional shifts. Include a declined request and two requests involving the same shift so the prototype exposes conflict handling. Defer accounts, automated matching, calendar integration, and polished styling.

Use the final half-day to seek an organizer walkthrough with an anonymized past email exchange, if one is available. Check whether they can identify the shift, confirm replacement consent, make a decision, and record the change without asking for missing information. If nobody is available, do a labeled internal walkthrough and treat usability and demand as unvalidated. The next larger investment should depend on that organizer feedback, rather than the prototype merely functioning.
