**Recommendation:** Invest next in a small, playable forecast-and-response loop on one board. Keep five boards as the intended scope, but decide how to build the other four after the first demonstrates a reason to plan differently when the forecast changes.

**Preserve:** Interesting choices created by changing forecasts, a solo-development scope, and no campaign. You do not need a campaign to explore this idea.

**Why:** Your brief gives the game a clear source of tactical tension and a bounded amount of content. That supports proceeding to a prototype. The decisive uncertainty is whether forecasts create competing, understandable choices: you have not yet specified what players can do, what preparation costs, or how weather affects an objective. If responding to each forecast has one obvious best answer—or changes simply invalidate earlier decisions—the central promise may not hold. Those are design risks, not findings from play.

**Next experiment:** Make a rough version of one board with one objective, two weather effects, and two or three competing actions. Use placeholder icons. Cap this first pass at two working days; if implementation takes longer, use a paper version to settle the rules first.

One possible test situation: a storm may close a short route. Players can take the longer safe route, spend a limited resource protecting the shortcut, or risk advancing while retaining that resource. Make the timing of forecast updates explicit. A changed forecast should leave some useful response available, even if previous preparation has a cost.

Use three short weather sequences on the same board. Record:

- Whether different forecasts make different actions attractive, and why.
- Whether players can anticipate a consequence before choosing and explain it afterward.
- Whether a forecast revision creates a fresh tradeoff or merely makes the previous turn feel wasted.

Start with your own walkthrough. If you can get a few people who enjoy tactics games to try it, let them play without coaching and note where their reasoning differs from yours. Your walkthrough can reveal rule problems; it cannot establish that others find the game interesting.

For this project, use this provisional decision rule: proceed to a second board if at least two weather sequences reward different plans and forecast revisions leave a consequential response. Revise the action costs or forecast timing if one strategy dominates or reacting is automatic. If the loop remains uninteresting after one focused revision, pause board production and reconsider the weather mechanic.

**Meaningful alternative:** Start with perfectly reliable forecasts whose time horizon advances each turn. This isolates the pleasure of planning and is easier to diagnose. Uncertain forecasts could add risk management later, but also require clearer communication and recovery options. Choose uncertainty now only if managing unreliable information is essential to your intended experience.
