**Recommendation:** Invest your next script session in a three-scene outline that makes mutual trust visible through choices before the final reveal. Keep the reveal provisionally; test whether it deepens those choices or does all the work of explaining them.

**Preserve:** The night bus encounter, the single location, and your focus on trust between strangers. There is no need to expand the production scope to explore this theme.

**Why proceed:** The premise offers a useful dramatic tension: two people must interpret each other with limited information. A confined encounter can let small actions and changes in confidence carry the story. That is a structural opportunity, not evidence that the unwritten scenes already deliver it.

**Main risk:** You say the final reveal “explains every action.” That could produce satisfying reinterpretation. It could also leave the first two scenes emotionally unreadable, or imply that apparent trust was actually certainty, manipulation, or a misunderstanding. The decisive question is whether each stranger makes a meaningful choice while still unsure of the other. A reveal can explain hidden circumstances while leaving those choices brave and consequential.

I have only read the brief, not a script or scene outline. Character motives, tone, dialogue, and the reveal itself remain unknown, so I would not recommend cutting or rewriting the ending yet.

For the next outline, give each scene a distinct change in the relationship:

1. **Scene one: a limited offer.** Establish what each stranger wants in the encounter and a plausible reason for caution. One takes a small, voluntary risk. Let the audience understand the immediate intention even if the backstory stays hidden.
2. **Scene two: a reason to withdraw.** Something makes that initial trust harder to sustain. The other stranger must choose how to respond, creating a consequence that neither can simply explain away. This makes the trust mutual rather than one person proving worthy of the other’s generosity.
3. **Scene three: knowledge and a final choice.** The reveal changes how an earlier action is understood. Give the characters a response to that knowledge so the relationship reaches a local payoff; the explanation alone need not be the climax.

This is a proposed structure, not a diagnosis of scenes I have not seen. The stakes can remain quiet and personal.

**Two viable directions:** Keep the comprehensive reveal if retrospective understanding is central to the intended experience; this requires careful groundwork so mystery does not obscure immediate motives. Alternatively, let the reveal answer the practical mystery while leaving one motive uncertain. That gives trust room to survive incomplete knowledge, but sacrifices some closure. Either can serve your theme.

**Next experiment — one session, capped at two hours:** Write a one-page outline in your current reveal-driven form, then a second version with the same characters, location, and ending but clearer immediate motives. For each pivotal action, record what the character wants, what they know, what they risk, and how the other person’s next choice changes. Read each outline once with its ending covered.

Use these project-specific decision rules: proceed to a full script if both strangers’ choices are intelligible before the reveal and the ending changes their meaning without cancelling their uncertainty. Revise the setup if only the ending makes their actions plausible. Revise the reveal if it removes the very risk that made their trust matter. Prefer the original if it passes those checks and the alternative merely adds explanation.
