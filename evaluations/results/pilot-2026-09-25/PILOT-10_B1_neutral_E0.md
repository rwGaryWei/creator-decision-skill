Your next investment should be a small playable test of the forecast mechanic: one board, a few actions, and placeholder icons. The idea has a clear focus, but the brief does not yet establish whether changing forecasts create interesting decisions or simply force players to revise plans.

For players who enjoy compact tactics and replaying scenarios, the appeal would be making a plan under uncertainty, then deciding when to commit, hedge, or adapt. That audience is an assumption to test. Players seeking fully calculable puzzles may find changing forecasts frustrating unless the uncertainty is visible and understandable.

The strongest parts of the concept are its limited scope and a mechanic that could change the value of familiar actions without requiring a large amount of content. Five boards and no campaign are sensible constraints for one developer. They do not, by themselves, establish enough variety: that depends on whether weather changes produce different viable plans on the same board.

The main design risk is that the forecast becomes either an instruction or a distraction. If rain always means “move uphill,” the choice is obvious. If forecasts change without useful warning, planning may feel pointless. The promising space is between those extremes: weather should create competing benefits, and forecasts should communicate what is known and what remains uncertain. For example, shelter might reduce storm exposure but surrender a valuable position; committing early might gain ground but make adaptation expensive. These are possible design directions, not evidence that the mechanic works.

For the next experiment:

1. Build one short scenario with two weather states and three competing actions—such as advance, secure shelter, or prepare for the next turn. Keep the objective and movement rules simple.
2. Make three versions of that same scenario: fixed weather, a fully reliable forecast, and a forecast that can visibly update within stated bounds. This separates the value of weather, advance information, and forecast uncertainty.
3. Have a handful of tactics players try the versions in different orders. Before key moves, ask what they expect and what alternative they considered. Afterwards, ask which forecast information mattered and whether a bad outcome felt understandable. Watch for choices they actually make, rather than relying only on whether they say the idea sounds fun.

A useful early signal would be players explaining distinct, reasonable plans and sometimes choosing a costlier hedge because of the forecast. Repeatedly choosing the same action, ignoring the forecast, or describing updates as arbitrary would point to a design revision. A small test gives directional evidence, not a reliable estimate of broader demand.

If the reliable forecast produces good decisions but updates do not, invest in making uncertainty clearer or less volatile. If neither adds meaningful choices over fixed weather, change the interaction between weather, position, and objectives before building more boards. Expand to the remaining four boards only when the first supports several defensible approaches.

AI icons keep the production task narrow and avoid runtime AI costs. They still require generation, selection, cleanup, consistent styling, and checks for readability at game size. Without an icon count or workflow, a meaningful cost estimate is not available. Use labeled shapes during the mechanic test; later, trial a small icon set and record both cash spend and total editing time. For now, learning whether the forecast earns its place will be more valuable than polishing its presentation.
