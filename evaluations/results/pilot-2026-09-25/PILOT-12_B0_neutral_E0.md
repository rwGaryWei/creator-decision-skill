Your next investment should be a small playtest of the exploration loop. The synthetic notes raise a useful question—whether backtracking feels rewarding—but they do not establish that real players have this problem.

Your goal is clear: reward exploration without making players exhaust dialogue. The main design risk is that players may feel obliged to revisit every location or conversation because they cannot tell what has changed. That would turn curiosity into checking chores. Backtracking can still serve the goal when returning reveals a meaningful change, a new route, or a discovery the player anticipated.

I would not prioritize a wholesale writing rewrite. “All AI writing is bad” provides no specific evidence about your dialogue’s clarity, character, or usefulness, and here it comes from a synthetic note. You need concrete examples before deciding what to change.

For the next work session:

1. Choose one short mailbox quest that includes exploration and a return trip. If there is no playable build, make a rough version of just that sequence.
2. Watch 3–5 people play it without coaching. Note where they return, what they expect to find, whether anything has changed, and whether they repeat dialogue to check for missed progress. This is an initial diagnostic, not a representative verdict.
3. Ask afterward: “When did you know where you wanted to go next?” and “Which discoveries felt worth the trip?”

If players repeatedly revisit unchanged places because they lack direction, test one small fix: a visible clue pointing toward the changed mailbox, a shortcut, or a persistent record of the last useful lead. Keep optional discoveries along the route. Compare whether players find their next step with fewer empty trips while still choosing to explore.

Based on the brief alone, the exploration goal is coherent; the execution remains unverified. Spend effort getting that evidence before expanding content or rewriting all the dialogue.
