The idea has a focused scope and a useful design question: can a forecast make players commit to a plan, hedge against risk, or change direction? Five boards and no campaign keep the content target modest, though the rules and opponent behavior are still undefined. At the idea stage, there is not enough evidence to judge whether the choices will be interesting.

The main risk is that changing weather produces one obvious response—or makes earlier decisions feel pointless. Forecasts become valuable when players can act before the weather arrives, and when preparing for one outcome costs them something elsewhere.

Your next investment should be a playable prototype of one board. Use paper or a plain grid with placeholder symbols. Defer the other four boards and generated icons until the forecast mechanic earns its place.

For the first experiment:

- Give the player one clear objective, such as reaching an extraction point within six turns.
- Use two routes with different weather exposure. For example, rain may slow the short route while the sheltered route always takes longer.
- Show a two-turn forecast and offer three competing responses: advance now, take shelter, or spend a limited resource to resist the weather. Make each response cost time, position, or that resource.
- Start with accurate forecasts. Once planning works, introduce clearly signaled uncertainty to test whether changing predictions add a worthwhile decision.

Run the board with a few people unfamiliar with the design. Before important moves, ask what they expect and which alternative they considered. Note whether the forecast changes their plan, whether two options seem defensible, and whether a revised forecast leaves them a meaningful response. Those observations matter more than a general “was it fun?” answer at this stage.

A useful milestone is seeing players explain different reasonable plans from the same position. If everyone makes the same move, strengthen the tradeoffs. If players ignore the forecast, make advance preparation more consequential. If they feel blindsided, improve the warning or their ability to recover.

Keep the commitment small: one board, one weather effect, one short playtest cycle. That will tell you whether to deepen the forecast rules before investing in content or presentation.
